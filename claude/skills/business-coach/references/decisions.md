# Testing ideas and making decisions

Read this when the founder brings a new idea, a feature, a priority call, or a decision such as
whether to launch, hire, pivot, spend or stop.

## Is there real evidence? (YC office hours)

Ask the questions that fit the stage.

- **No product yet:** What is the strongest evidence someone wants this: not interest, but upset
  if it disappeared? What do people do about it now, and what does that cost them? Name the one
  person who needs it most.
- **Has users:** What do people do now instead? What is the smallest version someone would pay
  for this week? Have you watched someone use it without helping, and what surprised you?
- **Has paying customers:** What is the smallest paid version? What did watching users teach you?
  If the world changes a lot in three years, does this become more essential or less?

"Nothing like it exists" is a warning sign, not an advantage: it often means nobody cares enough to
have tried.

**The exception: a genuinely new category.** Both this rule and the Mom Test's "if they have not
looked for a solution, they will not buy yours" give false negatives when the thing is new enough
that nobody knew to look. There, stated need is weak evidence either way. Test behaviour instead:
show something concrete and see what people do with it, and ask what they currently put up with
rather than whether they have searched for a fix. Do not kill a new category on interviews alone.

## Asking customers without being lied to (Rob Fitzpatrick, The Mom Test)

- Talk about their life, not your idea. Ask about specific past events, not opinions about the
  future. Listen more than you talk.
- Good questions: "Talk me through the last time that happened." "What else have you tried?"
  "How are you dealing with it now?" "What does that cost you?" "Who else should I talk to?"
- Do not ask "Would you buy this?", "How much would you pay?" or "Do you think it's a good idea?"
  People answer politely and the answers are worthless.
- Discount compliments, vague generalities ("I always", "I would") and feature ideas.
- If they have not looked for a solution already, they will not buy yours.
- End every conversation with a commitment: time, an introduction, or money (a deposit or
  pre-order). "Let me know when it launches" is a polite no.

## Why people switch (jobs to be done: Bob Moesta, Clayton Christensen)

- People switch when the push away from the old way plus the pull of the new one outweigh anxiety
  about the new one plus the habit of the old one. Founders see push and pull; customers rarely
  volunteer anxiety and habit, so ask for them.
- Interview people who recently switched, not prospects. Walk the timeline: when did they first
  think the old way was not working, and what triggered it? When did they start looking, then
  comparing? What tipped the decision and what nearly stopped it? Did the first experience match
  what they expected?
- **No switchers yet?** Interview people who recently switched to the nearest alternative, or who
  recently changed how they deal with the problem at all. Their timeline shows the pushes and
  anxieties you will meet.

## Deciding under uncertainty

- **What if you did nothing?** The BRAIN checklist Ferriss cited: Benefits, Risks, Alternatives,
  Intuition, Nothing.
- **Fear-setting (Ferriss):** the worst case, how to prevent it, how to repair it; the benefit of
  trying, even partly; the cost of not acting at six months, one year and three years.
- **Can it be undone?** (Bezos's one-way and two-way doors.) Decisions that are cheap to reverse
  should be made fast, with about 70% of the information you would like. Decisions that are hard
  to reverse deserve slow, deliberate thought.
- **Pre-mortem (Annie Duke):** imagine it failed a year from now and list the most likely reasons.
  Then decide which of those you can test or prevent now.
- **Kill criteria (Annie Duke):** set in advance a state and a date: "If by [date] we have not
  reached [state], we stop." Decide this before starting, when you are not yet attached.
- **Would I start this today?** Knowing what you know now. If not, what keeps you going is probably
  sunk cost.
- **Decision journal (Farnam Street):** write down the situation, the options rejected, the
  expected outcome with rough odds, and your state of mind. Review later to separate a bad
  decision from bad luck.
- **Lead domino (Ferriss):** of all the things on the list, which one, once done, makes the others
  easier or unnecessary?
- **"What would this look like if it were easy?" (Ferriss):** useful when a plan has grown heavy.

## Build less (37signals, Getting Real)

- Half a product beats a half-finished product.
- Fix the time and budget; cut scope to fit.
- Build what you would use yourself, where that applies.

## Tests to propose

- Five Mom Test interviews with people who fit the buyer profile, ending each with a request for a
  commitment. Worked if at least two commit (time, intro or money).
- A fake-door test: a button or page for the feature that records clicks before it exists. Say on
  click that it is not built yet and offer to tell them when it is; a door that leads nowhere
  without explanation costs trust. Clicks are weak evidence, so treat them as a reason to run a
  stronger test. Stop after a fixed number of visitors.
- A pre-sale or deposit for the smallest paid version. Worked if a set number pay by a set date.
  Decide before starting what happens to the money if the stop rule trips.
- A written pre-mortem and kill criteria before any spend over a set amount.
