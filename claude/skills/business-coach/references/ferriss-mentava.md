## The business

- An iPad phonics app for children aged roughly 2 to 5, aiming at a second-grade reading level.
  Built on game design: motivation is a hoop, so make the hoop smaller or the reward bigger.
- Price: $500 a month per child, with a 14-day free trial. Buyers are, in Niels's words, "fairly
  wealthy".
- An unadvertised $100-a-month scholarship for families who love it and ask for help, in return
  for a weekly video of the child using the app (marketing material).
- A readiness check inside the trial (the "dog-fish, fish-dog" test); children who are not ready
  are told to try again in 3 to 6 months.
- Word of mouth and community far outperform paid ads.

## Each challenge, generalised

### 1. Words that shrink the market

- **Mentava:** copy leaned on "unusually fast learner", "gifted", "top 1%". Tim pushed back; a
  child need not be a genius to benefit. The bio moved to "eager kids". (Transcript and recap;
  "eager kids" is in the live show notes.)
- **Why it matters:** a label meant to flatter the ideal buyer tells everyone else the product is
  not for them, and many buyers do not see themselves in an elite label even when they fit.
- **Check:** list every word that describes the buyer. For each, ask who reads it and decides
  "not me".
- **Test:** run two versions of the headline to the same audience (an ad, an email subject line,
  a post) and compare click or reply rates.

### 2. A message that makes the buyer feel judged

- **Mentava:** Tim warned that parents might read the pitch as "I'm just trying to foist the
  responsibility of education onto an app. I'm a terrible parent." His reframe was relief: "you
  don't have to nag, you don't have to police, you don't have to reward, you don't have to give
  cookies." (Confirmed, transcript.) Mentava's current tagline is "Faster learning. Fewer battles.
  Better time together."
- **Why it matters:** if buying means admitting a failing, people delay or refuse, whatever the
  product does.
- **Check:** what does buying say about the buyer? If the answer is unflattering, rewrite around
  the pain removed and the time or peace gained.

### 3. Best proof below the fold

- **Mentava:** the "love letters" section sat below the fold on a laptop. Tim picked out a parent's
  line about more progress in one week than in about six months of kindergarten: surprising, but
  "not so bananas as to be outside of the realm of possibility". (Confirmed, transcript.) Advice:
  lead with the testimonials.
- **Why it matters:** a specific, believable customer sentence does more than any claim the
  founder makes, and most visitors never scroll.
- **Check:** find the single most specific customer quote. Is it visible without scrolling?
- **Test:** move it into the hero area for two weeks and compare trial or sign-up rate.

### 4. Paying for referrals when sharing costs social standing

- **Mentava:** every customer got a code giving a free month to both sides, worth about $1,000 per
  pair. Zero referrals, even though sales calls showed word of mouth was happening. The explanation
  (attributed to Niels): for wealthy customers, social standing, not money, drives sharing, and
  recommending an expensive children's product carries social risk. (Transcript and recap.)
- **Why it matters:** a referral reward only works if money is the missing motive. If the barrier
  is embarrassment, risk or looking like a salesperson, money makes it worse.
- **Check:** what does the customer risk, socially, by recommending you? What would make
  recommending you look generous, informed or kind?
- **Test:** give customers something that makes them look good to share (a result, a report, an
  invitation they can extend), and cap any paid referral trial in advance, for example the first
  100 referrers only, so the worst-case cost is known. (Cap: recap.)

### 5. Filtering customers too late

- **Mentava:** the readiness check happened inside the trial, so unready families used up a trial
  and left disappointed. The suggestion was a two-minute readiness quiz on the homepage: it
  captures an email, filters out children who are not ready, and gives parents a result they can
  share. (Recap.)
- **Why it matters:** a filter at the front becomes a lead magnet and a follow-up list; a filter
  at the back becomes churn.
- **Check:** where in the journey do you discover a customer is a bad fit? Could a short quiz or
  self-assessment surface it before they start?

### 6. The cost of doing nothing is invisible

- **Mentava:** Tim brought in the BRAIN checklist he learned from a doula: Benefits, Risks,
  Alternatives, Intuition, Nothing. He noted it was the first checklist he had seen with "what if
  you did nothing?" as a prompt. (Confirmed, transcript.)
- **Why it matters:** the buyer's real competitor is usually inaction. If the page does not make
  the cost of waiting concrete, waiting wins.
- **Check:** for the buyer, and separately for the founder's own decision, what does doing nothing
  cost in six months and in a year?

### 7. Exaggeration is death

- **Mentava:** discussed against viral posts and the culture-war trap. "Exaggeration is death. Do
  not exaggerate." One casual claim that can be proven false casts doubt on every other claim, and
  competitors will use it. (Confirmed, transcript.)
- **Check:** mark every claim a sceptic could test. Cut it, soften it to what is true, or add the
  source.

### 8. Put the ask on top

- **Mentava:** in investor updates, the request goes first; Tim receives many updates a month.
  (Chapter title confirmed; detail recap.)
- **General rule:** whatever you want the reader to do goes where they will see it first: the top
  of an email, the hero of a page, the first line of a post.

### 9. Uncrowded channels, and workshop the message live

- **Mentava:** speak at community events, target specific customer types, test the message the way
  a stand-up comedian workshops a set. One example venue was an entrepreneur network whose members
  already understand the problem. (Chapter title confirmed; venue detail recap.)
- **Why it matters:** attention is cheapest where competitors are not, and a live room tells you
  within seconds which line lands.
- **Check:** where does your buyer gather that competitors ignore? Could you deliver the pitch
  live to ten people this month and note which lines get a reaction?

### 10. Do not let the big launch be the first test

- **Mentava:** "Don't let Amazon be your debutante ball: testing what sells." (Chapter title
  only; the context is probably a book or product launch.)
- **General rule:** find out what sells with small, cheap tests (posts, ads, emails, talks) before
  a high-stakes launch decides it for you in public.

## Tim's wider habits (inferred from his other work)

- **Lead domino:** "Which one of these, if done, would make everything else easier or
  irrelevant?" Well documented in his books and podcast.
- **"What would this look like if it were easy?"** A reframe for a plan that has become heavy.
- **Fear-setting:** define the worst case, how to prevent it and how to repair it; then list the
  benefits of trying and the cost of inaction at six months, one year and three years.
- **Test before building:** his early practice of testing book titles and product demand with
  cheap ads before committing.
- **Edit the founder's own words live**, rather than describing the principle. This one is
  confirmed in episode 882.
