# Growth and first users

Read this when the founder brings a growth plan, a channel question, a referral scheme, a launch,
or the question "how do I get my first customers?".

## Two-sided businesses

Marketplaces, directories, review sites and platforms have two groups to win, and often only one
of them pays (or neither, yet). Before applying anything below:

- Name each side, what each gets, and who pays.
- Find the side that is the bottleneck now. Usually it is supply first (enough listings, sellers
  or brands to be worth a visit), then demand.
- The channel-and-price check applies only to the side that pays. The other side needs a reason
  to show up that is not money: something useful, something flattering to share, or something it
  cannot get elsewhere.
- A plan that reaches one side and assumes the other will follow is the most common gap. Ask how
  the first hundred of each side arrive, separately.

## Start with the diagnostic question

"Explain, step by step, how your last ten customers found you and decided to buy." (Alex Hormozi
asks a version of this.) Vague answers ("word of mouth", "social") mean nobody has looked yet;
the first task is to find out, usually by asking those customers.

## Does the channel fit the price? (Brian Balfour, four fits)

- Balfour wrote this for companies aiming at $100 million or more in a venture-backed time frame.
  The questions still help a small business; the targets do not.
- Products are built to fit channels; channels do not bend to fit products.
- **Channel and price:** can the revenue from one customer pay for what the channel costs to win
  one? Low prices force cheap channels: word of mouth, search, virality, community. High prices
  can afford sales calls, events and paid ads.
- **Model and market:** price × customers you can realistically reach has to reach the revenue
  target. A higher price needs fewer customers.
- Change one of these and the others move. A price change is also a channel decision.

## Master one channel first (Alex Hormozi, $100M Leads)

Four ways to reach people:

|                     | One to one    | One to many |
| ------------------- | ------------- | ----------- |
| People who know you | Warm outreach | Content     |
| Strangers           | Cold outreach | Paid ads    |

- Start warm, then get good at one channel before adding another.
- The "rule of 100": 100 outreaches, or 100 minutes of content, or 100 of currency in ads, every
  day for 100 days. The point is volume and consistency, not the exact number. It assumes a
  full-time founder: scale it to the hours they actually have, and keep the consistency.
- Lead magnets come in three kinds: reveal a problem the buyer has, give a sample or trial, or
  solve one step of the problem. A quiz or readiness check is the first kind.
- Other people can bring leads: customers, partners, affiliates, communities. Ask what is in it
  for them, and money is not always the answer (see referrals below).

## Do things that don't scale (Paul Graham, YC)

- Recruit the first users by hand, one at a time. Delight each one.
- Build for a few people who want it a lot, rather than many who want it a little.
- Measure growth weekly. Graham's bar, 5 to 7% a week, is for venture-funded software aiming to
  be very large. It is not a health check for a small or bootstrapped business, where 1% a week
  (about 68% a year) is excellent. Below a few hundred customers, weekly rates are mostly noise:
  watch the count and the reasons, not the percentage.

## How consumer products got their first 1,000 users (Lenny Rachitsky)

Seven routes: go where users are offline; go where they are online; invite friends; create a
reason to talk about it; influencers; press; build a community before launch. Most products used
exactly one route, none more than three. Pick one.

## Referrals and word of mouth (from the Mentava session)

- A $1,000 two-sided reward produced zero referrals, while word of mouth was clearly happening.
  For some buyers, recommending a product is a social risk, and money does not remove that risk.
- Ask: what does the customer risk by recommending you? What would make recommending you look
  generous, informed or kind?
- Give customers something worth sharing for its own sake: a result, a report, a before and after,
  an invitation they control.
- Cap any paid referral test in advance (the first 100 referrers, a fixed budget) so the worst
  case is known.

## Uncrowded channels (from the Mentava session)

- Go where the buyer gathers and competitors are absent: a professional network, a local group, a
  niche newsletter, a conference with the right people and no rivals on stage.
- Speaking to a room doubles as message testing: note which lines get a reaction and rewrite.

## Bootstrapped and small businesses (Rob Walling, 37signals)

- Walling's stair step: start with a small product sold through one channel with built-in
  discovery (an app store, a marketplace, a plugin directory), repeat until it pays the bills, and
  only then build a larger recurring product.
- Fix time and budget, and cut scope to fit. Sell what you learn along the way (content, guides,
  tools) as its own channel.

## Tests to propose

- 20 warm messages to people who fit the buyer profile, asking for a 15-minute call. Worked if
  five accept.
- One talk or workshop at a gathering of the buyer group this month. Worked if it produces a set
  number of sign-ups or conversations.
- A short quiz or check as a lead magnet on the homepage. Worked if it captures a set share of
  visitors' emails in four weeks.
- A capped referral variant (non-cash, shareable result) to the existing customer base. Stop at the
  cap or after four weeks.
