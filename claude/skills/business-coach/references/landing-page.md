# Landing pages and messaging

Read this when the founder brings a page, a headline, a tagline, a bio, a pitch or a post.

## Order of work

1. **Five-second read.** Look only at what is visible without scrolling on a laptop. Write down
   what a stranger would say the product is, who it is for, and what to do next. If you cannot
   answer all three, that is the finding, and everything else waits.
2. **Walk the page top to bottom** with the checks below.
3. **Pick the one change with the biggest effect** and red-pen it: original line, rewrite, one
   line on why.
4. **Propose a test** that would show whether the change works.

## Checks

### Clarity (Peep Laja, CXL)

- Can a visitor tell where they are, what they can do here, and why here rather than elsewhere?
- "Clarity trumps persuasion." A clever headline that needs decoding loses to a plain one.
- Score the page on relevance, clarity, value, friction and distraction. Name the worst one.

### Is each line worth its place? (Harry Dry, Marketing Examples)

For each important line ask:

- Can I picture it?
- Could it be proven false? (A line nobody could disprove says nothing.)
- Could nobody else say it? (If a competitor could paste it onto their page, it is filler.)

Three "no" answers means the line should go.

### Positioning (April Dunford, Obviously Awesome)

- What would the buyer use if you did not exist? That is the real comparison, and often it is
  doing nothing or doing it by hand.
- What do you have that those alternatives do not, and can you prove it?
- What can the buyer do because of that? That is the value, and it belongs in the headline.
- Who cares most about that value? Write for them, not for everyone.
- Which category makes your value obvious at a glance?
- Signs of a positioning problem: prospects compare you to things you are not; sales calls are
  spent explaining what you are; you cannot charge a premium.
- Dunford built this for B2B software, and says so. It fits a business buyer well. For a consumer,
  the alternative is usually a habit rather than a competitor, and the pull is often identity and
  reassurance ("people like me do this") more than features. On a two-sided product, use it for the
  business side and lean on the customer's own words for the consumer side.

### The customer's own words (Joanna Wiebe, Copyhackers)

- Take headline language from real customers: reviews, interviews, emails, forum posts, sales-call
  notes. "Join the conversation already happening in the customer's head."
- Never invent these words. If the founder has none, the first task is to collect them.

### The Ferriss checks (from the Mentava session)

- Do any words describing the buyer shrink the market or make them feel judged?
- Is the most specific, believable testimonial visible without scrolling?
- Is there any claim a sceptic could prove false?
- Does the page make the cost of doing nothing concrete?
- Is the thing you want the visitor to do at the top?
- Does the page filter out bad-fit visitors early, ideally with something they can take away (a
  quiz, a check, a result)?

### Value (Alex Hormozi's value equation, used as a page check)

Value = (dream outcome × how likely the buyer believes it is) ÷ (time until it happens × effort
and sacrifice).

- Dream outcome: is it concrete and measurable, or vague ("grow", "improve")?
- Likelihood: is there proof and a visible mechanism, or only promises?
- Time: is there a quick first win?
- Effort: does the page make the buyer's work look small?

Fix the weakest term first. Low trust: add proof. Drop-off: reduce effort. Low motivation: shorten
the time to a first result.

## A sensible section order

Not a rule, but a default to compare against:

1. Headline and subhead: the outcome, for whom.
2. The strongest proof (a specific customer line, a number, a logo).
3. The problem, in the customer's words, including the cost of doing nothing.
4. How it works, in three steps or fewer.
5. More proof.
6. Objections answered (price, time, "will it work for me", risk).
7. Price, after the value is clear.
8. The call to action, repeated after the hero, after the proof and at the end.
9. FAQ.

Say who it is not for. It builds trust with the people it is for.

## Cheap tests for a page

Most early pages have too little traffic to compare versions reliably; see the small-numbers rule
in `SKILL.md`, step 5. The tests below find confusion and weak lines. Few of them can rank two good
versions.

- **Five-second test:** show the hero to five people from the target group for five seconds, then
  ask what it is and who it is for. It shows whether people understand the page, not which of two
  versions converts better. Cost: an afternoon.
- **Headline test through ads:** two headlines, small budget, compare click-through. Use it to drop
  lines that clearly lose, not to pick the page headline: ad platforms show each version to
  different people, and clicks on an ad are not visits that convert. Stop after a fixed spend, not
  when a winner "feels" clear.
- **Say it live:** deliver the pitch to a room or on calls and note which line gets a reaction.
- **Move the proof up:** put the best testimonial in the hero. Unless traffic clears the
  small-numbers rule, treat this as a change to watch, not a test: two different fortnights differ
  for many reasons besides the page.
- **Ask five customers** "what almost stopped you from buying?" and check the page answers it.
