# Offer and pricing

Read this when the founder brings a question about what to sell, how to package it, what to charge,
or what guarantee to give. Draws mainly on Alex Hormozi's $100M Offers, with the hype removed.

## The value equation

Value = (dream outcome × how likely the buyer believes it is) ÷ (time until it happens × effort
and sacrifice).

- Weak dream outcome: vague verbs, nothing measurable.
- Weak likelihood: no proof, no visible process.
- Long time delay: no quick first win.
- High effort: too many steps for the buyer.

Fix in this order: the outcome first; then proof if trust is low; effort if people drop off; time
if motivation fades.

## Building an offer

1. Pick a buyer specific enough that the pain is the same across them. Test the market first:
   is the pain urgent, can they pay, can you reach them, is the group growing?
2. Write the dream outcome in their words.
3. List every obstacle between them and that outcome (aim for ten or more).
4. Turn each obstacle into something the offer does about it.
5. Decide how it is delivered: they do it themselves, you do it with them, or you do it for them.
   Price rises in that order.
6. Say who it is not for.

## Pricing

- Anchor the price to what the outcome is worth to the buyer (money made, time saved, pain
  avoided), not to your costs.
- Know the floor as well as the anchor: the price must cover what it costs to deliver and to win
  each customer, with margin left. Ask for those two numbers; do not guess them.
- Price up when the outcome is valuable, fast and low-effort for the buyer. Price down, or start
  simpler, when the outcome is uncertain or you have no proof yet.
- Stress test: if it feels cheap, trust is probably low; if it feels expensive, the value is
  probably unclear.
- A high price can itself be a signal of seriousness. A search summary of Niels Hoven's writing
  on Mentava's $500 monthly price says some buyers took it as a sign the company was not a scam
  (unverified; see `sources.md`).

## Guarantees

- Unconditional (money back), conditional (money back if they did the work), outcome-based (tied
  to a result), or none ("all sales final", sometimes a signal of confidence at high prices;
  check consumer cancellation rights when building it).
- Choose to fit the price and the trust level. Only offer what you would honour cheerfully.

## Objections

- Each surface objection hides a belief: "too expensive" usually means "not sure it is worth it";
  "I'll think about it" usually means "I don't trust it yet".
- Group them: value, trust, effort, time, identity, risk.
- If an objection is valid, fix the offer, not the copy.

## What to leave out

The Hormozi material also recommends tactics this skill does not: fake scarcity, deadline bonuses
that are not real, inflated "total value" figures, charm pricing presented as insight, and the goal
of making prospects "feel stupid saying no". They win a sale and cost trust, and trust is what a
small business compounds. If the founder asks about them, say so plainly.
